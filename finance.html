Battery debt financing

How much a lender could lend against one generic leased home battery, and against a fleet, from the cash the battery earns, after the member's payment and the federal storage tax credit, and whether the battery pays back at all. Change any input. The page redoes the math as you type.

A generic model, not any company's books. This site is not affiliated with Base Power or any battery company. The defaults are generic: installed cost from NREL's public cost benchmark, statewide retail and delivery prices from EIA, ERCOT prices, and labeled assumptions for the lease terms, lender terms, the toll and operating cost. One company's published figures are available as an optional preset, labeled as that company's public claims, not its economics.

What drives the default answer: a retail energy margin (derived from EIA and ERCOT data with one labeled cost assumption), ERCOT energy arbitrage (derived from public prices with assumptions) and member fees (an assumption) carry the loan, and the 30% tax credit, sold at a market discount, cuts what must be funded. The default assumes the battery's owner is also the home's retail provider, which only exists in zones with retail choice; a third-party owner should set the retail margin to 0. The default case is merchant, so the utility toll placeholder does not count. On the generic defaults the battery does not pay back; "Does it pay?" says by how much and what is left out.

Each input says Sourced (public number with a link, sometimes with our stated math), Assumption (our placeholder, or our math with a labeled assumption) or, after you load the preset, Company claim (a figure a company published about itself).

Inputs, per battery

What earns the cash

A toll is a contract in which a utility pays the battery owner for the right to dispatch the battery, usually on a pay-for-performance basis. Whoever holds the toll usually controls dispatch, so toll and arbitrage rarely both go to the owner. In a city-utility or co-op zone (no retail choice) a utility contract like this is the only way a battery company shares the battery's value.

Energy arbitrage only, 12 real ERCOT months, from our zone value file.

The arbitrage value is derived from public ERCOT prices with assumptions, not a published number: a fixed charge and discharge clock (10 AM to 2 PM in, 7 to 9 PM out), 88% round trip efficiency and a 20% backup reserve.

Zones without retail choice. LZ_AEN (Austin Energy), LZ_CPS (CPS Energy, San Antonio), LZ_LCRA (LCRA's wholesale customers, mostly cities and co-ops) and LZ_RAYBN (Rayburn Country Electric Cooperative) are what ERCOT calls Non-Opt-In Entity load zones (Protocols 3.4.3; ERCOT board item 13.2, December 2025). Texas law leaves retail choice to each city utility (PURA 40.051) and co-op board (PURA 41.051), and these have not opted in. No retail provider exists there to keep the arbitrage or a retail margin: the value goes to the utility, so the model counts neither and points to the toll case. The other four zones are mostly retail choice, with some city and co-op pockets.

Who keeps the export value. At up to 20 kW from 7 to 9 PM, most of the discharge is more than a home uses, so it flows out through the meter. In ERCOT, a home battery of 1 MW or less that is not registered with ERCOT is an Unregistered Distributed Generator (the Nodal Protocols' Section 2 counts energy storage as distributed generation). Its metered outflow is subtracted from the settlement load of the home's retail provider (Nodal Protocols 11.4.4.3), and the provider pays for that load at the real-time load-zone price (6.6.3.2). So:

  • (a) The owner is the home's retail provider (its REP and load-serving entity). Every kWh the battery discharges, used at home or exported, cuts that provider's load-zone purchases at the load-zone price, and every kWh it charges adds to them. The arbitrage line is kept through the provider's own settlement. No ADER aggregation is needed for it.
  • (b) The owner is a third party, not the retail provider. The same cut lands on whichever provider serves the home. The owner gets the value only by contract with that provider, or by registering the battery with ERCOT as a settlement-only resource through a QSE. Texas rule 16 TAC 25.217(f)(2) lets owners of renewable distributed generation sell outflow to their REP "at a value to which both parties agree"; we found no rule that makes a REP buy battery outflow at any price.
  • (c) ERCOT's ADER pilot is the route to ancillary services (Non-Spin and ECRS so far) and to dispatch as an aggregated resource. It is not needed for the energy value in (a).

Not settled by these sources: the reduction in 11.4.4.3 is written for premises the wires utility has flagged as having distributed generation (a DG profile segment), so it depends on the battery being reported at interconnection; and distribution loss factors shift the value by a few percent. Not modeled.

Per battery

Does it pay?

The whole battery (unlevered): pay the net cost in year 0, get CFADS every year of life. Equity: put in the equity, get CFADS after loan payments. NPV at the discount rate input. No taxes, depreciation, residual value or escalators.

        Method and what is left out

        • Debt sizing. Level yearly payment over the loan term. The payment is the weakest year's CFADS inside the term, divided by the target DSCR. Debt is the present value of that payment at the interest rate. Then the loan is capped at the advance rate times installed cost, and never above cost minus the member's upfront payment.
        • Tax credit. The federal investment tax credit for energy storage (IRC 48E) goes to the owner of a leased battery. The model treats it the simplest standard way: the owner sells the credit for cash when the battery goes into service (a transfer under IRC 6418), at the credit sale price (default 89.5 cents per dollar, Crux's 2026-vintage ITC average), and that cash lowers the net cost that debt and equity must fund. That is a simplification. Real lenders usually fund the credit separately, with a bridge loan sized on the credit and repaid when it sells, and size the term loan on cash flow and an advance rate on the full installed cost. The advance cap here already applies to the full cost, so the credit only changes the debt when the net-cost cap binds; when it does, equity shows $0 and the page says why. Left out: the months between paying for the battery and getting paid for the credit, bridge loan interest, five-year recapture risk and the insurance buyers ask for, the depreciation basis cut, bonus adders, and tax equity partnerships.
        • Equity per battery = installed cost minus member upfront payment minus tax credit minus debt.
        • Does it pay? The whole battery: pay the net cost (installed cost minus the member payment and the tax-credit cash) in year 0, get CFADS each year of its life. Unlevered IRR, NPV at the discount rate input (10% by default, a placeholder) and the payback year. Equity: put in the equity, get CFADS minus the loan payment. When the cash never covers the net cost the page says so plainly. Left out, any of which could change the answer: price spikes outside the fixed 7 to 9 PM window, ancillary services ($0 by default), depreciation, the battery's value after its useful life, fee escalators and a utility toll. We do not tune inputs to make it pay.
        • Tax credit size risk. The 30% rate without the wage and apprenticeship tests rests on each battery being a facility under 1 MW. Treas. Reg. 1.48E-3(c)(4)(ii) counts storage units as one facility for this test when they have the same or related owners, the same placed-in-service year and a shared point of interconnection or end user. Batteries at separate homes usually share neither, but common ownership, financing and aggregated dispatch are a risk to check with tax counsel, not a conclusion.
        • Fade cuts toll and arbitrage each year. Member fees stay flat. No inflation on anything.
        • Energy arbitrage is derived from public prices with assumptions, not a published number. It uses a fixed daily clock, 88% round trip efficiency and a 20% backup reserve: charge 10 AM to 2 PM, discharge 7 to 9 PM, Central time, every day, settled at the load zone real-time price. The value comes from the zone value file, which shows the schedule and method. Most of that discharge is export. For an unregistered battery of 1 MW or less, ERCOT settles outflow as a cut to the settlement load of the home's retail provider at the real-time load-zone price (Nodal Protocols Section 2, definitions of Distributed Generator and Unregistered Distributed Generator; 11.4.4.3, load reduction for excess from other distributed generators; 6.6.3.2(1)(g), Adjusted Metered Load charged at the load-zone price). So the full line belongs to an owner that is the home's retail provider (case a above). A third-party owner (case b) earns it only through a contract with that provider or by registering the battery with ERCOT (a Settlement Only Distribution Generator, 6.6.3.8, whose storage-majority sites keep load-zone pricing under 6.6.3.2(1)(j)). ADER (case c) adds ancillary services, not this energy value. No capacity value. It is not any company's revenue.
        • Retail energy margin defaults to $63 a month: (16.66 cents, what retail providers billed ERCOT homes in 2024 - 5.21 cents delivery charges billed by the wires utilities - 3.70 cents load-weighted LZ_NORTH wholesale price - 2.0 cents other supply cost, an assumption) x 1,097 kWh a month, all from EIA-861 except the wholesale price (our math on ERCOT data). It is a gross margin, before the provider's own cost to serve, so treat it as the high end. It uses the home's whole usage before the battery, and the arbitrage line values the battery's own charge and discharge at the same load-zone price, so the two add up without counting a kWh twice. It belongs only to an owner that is the home's retail provider, and only where retail choice exists; set it to 0 for a third-party owner.
        • Ancillary services (ERCOT reserves such as Non-Spin and ECRS, reached by home batteries only through the ADER pilot, where ECRS was fully subscribed in September 2026) are one input in $ per kW-year, $0 by default because no public number gives what an aggregated home battery nets. A battery cannot sell the same kWh twice: a kW held as reserve in an hour cannot also discharge for arbitrage that hour. So the input is the extra value after the arbitrage it displaces (for example, reserve sold in the 22 hours outside the discharge window), and the model adds it to arbitrage. It counts in the merchant and stacked cases, not the toll case, with availability and fade applied.
        • Inputs are checked the same way on this page and in the API: plain decimal numbers within each field's limits. A blank field keeps its default. An invalid field shows an error and the results wait until it is fixed.
        • Depreciation is not modeled. It is a tax deduction, not cash the battery earns, so it does not change CFADS or the debt sized on CFADS; it changes the owner's taxes and so the equity return, which "Does it pay?" shows before taxes.
        • Not modeled: income taxes, tax equity, member churn and defaults, reserve accounts, fees on the loan, sculpted payments, refinancing, and the value of the battery after the loan ends. Each of these can move the answer a lot.
        • Fleet is the unit result times a count. Real lenders look at the whole pool, with concentration limits and performance history.
